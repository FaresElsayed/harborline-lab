You are Harborline Logistics’ first data engineer. Over eight projects and about 32 weeks you build the company’s data platform and, on top of it, the dashboards each department has been asking for. This page is the brief you would get on day one.

Harborline Logistics

Harborline is a third-party logistics company: about sixty brands pay it to store their inventory, pick and pack their orders, ship them through the right carrier, and handle their returns. It grew from one Atlanta warehouse into eight sites in the United States, Canada and Mexico, three legal entities and four currencies.

The systems grew with it, one at a time. Operations runs a legacy on-prem WMS. Finance closes the books in Microsoft Dynamics AX 2012. Sales and Client Success live in Salesforce. Carriers, sensors and B2B partners each talk to Harborline in their own format. Every department builds its own Excel reports, and the numbers rarely agree. Nobody owns data. That is why you are here.

Your role

You report to Nadia Petrov, the data engineering team lead, who sets priorities with you and reviews your designs and pull requests. She reports to Sam Okafor, Director of IT, who sponsors the program and approves access and spend. Each project has a requester in the business who owns its outcomes and its metric definitions. You own the platform, the pipelines, the models and the dashboards. Nadia will challenge your thinking but won’t build them for you; everyone else can tell you what they need and how their systems behave.

  • Eight projects of about four weeks each at 40 hours a week, two two-week sprints per project.
  • Every ticket is already written in the Tracker, with acceptance criteria and a due date counted from your start date.
  • Every project ends in dashboards, a sign-off from Iris Novak, a release tag and a short readout to the requester.
  • The source systems keep changing while you work: new orders every simulated minute, updates, deletes, schema releases, late data and the odd incident.

Systems landscape

Rules of engagement

These make the exercise realistic. Breaking them makes it easier and teaches less.

  • Salesforce and carrier tracking are SaaS. Use their APIs with the credentials you were given, and respect their limits. There is no database to read.
  • Never read sim_internal. Your login cannot, and the admin password in .env is not yours to use. Treat the simulator’s code and configuration (simulator/, config/) as a vendor’s black box: learn the business from the data, this handbook and your colleagues.
  • Read-only on every source. The one write you are allowed is the client health score and churn risk pushed to Salesforce in Project 5.
  • The answer key waits. Open a project’s spoilers only after you have shipped that project’s dashboards. Score your data-quality findings whenever you like.
  • Iris signs off before anyone else sees a dashboard. Submit each dashboard to Iris Novak (how) and fix what she finds before the readout. Argue a finding with evidence, not opinion, and don’t read her working: checking your own work is your job.
  • Definitions belong to the business. When two people define a metric differently, you do not pick one. You bring the conflict to the owners, get a decision and write it down.
  • Every change goes through a pull request that names a ticket. No direct pushes to main, no changes made by hand in production.
  • No secrets or consumer personal data in Git, screenshots or demos. Mask ship-to names and addresses in anything you publish.
  • Spend nothing without a written case. Free tiers first; any cost goes to Sam as a short ADR.
  • Incidents are part of the job. When a scenario breaks your pipeline, fix it forward and write a short postmortem; do not reset the simulator to make it go away.

How the work gets harder

The tickets start easy and get harder on purpose. In your first week you run one query and open one small pull request. By Project 8 someone describes a business problem and the method is entirely yours. The help fades as you learn, the way a good team brings a new engineer up to speed.

Every story, task, spike and bug carries one label, level-1 to level-5. To see them in the Tracker, search labels = level-1.

LevelWhat the ticket asksWhat it gives you
1Follow a clear instruction in one systemThe exact thing to do, a known result, and a Where to start: the table or page to open first, and who to ask
2Build something small to a clear specThe spec, and a Where to start
3Join two or three systems and make a choiceThe goal and who to talk to. The method is mostly yours
4Design something with trade-offs and failure modes, such as change data capture, re-runs and late dataThe problem and the constraints. You defend the design
5Own a business outcome end to end, including security, stakeholders and the readoutA stakeholder’s ask, often with people who disagree. You work out what done means, with them

Less detail later on is deliberate. When a level-5 ticket leaves things out, it isn’t badly written and you haven’t missed a page. Senior work arrives that way: a person with a problem, definitions that conflict, edge cases nobody has thought about. Ask whoever owns the decision, write down what they said, and defend the rest yourself. Acceptance criteria stay checkable at every level, so you can always tell when you’re done.

Project 1 stays between levels 1 and 3, and its first sprint is mostly levels 1 and 2. Projects 2 to 4 sit mostly at levels 2 and 3, Projects 5 and 6 mostly at levels 3 and 4, and Projects 7 and 8 mostly at level 4, with more and more of level 5. Each project starts a little easier than the last one finished, because it opens a new part of the business, and then climbs. If you’re stuck, take it to Nadia before you’ve lost a day. She will challenge your thinking; asking is part of the job at every level.

Git and engineering standards

These are part of the Definition of Done on every ticket. They are what a reviewer looks at first in a portfolio repository.

GitHub flow

  • main is always releasable and protected. Nobody pushes to it, including you.
  • One short-lived branch per ticket, cut from the latest main, merged within a few days.
  • Every change lands through a pull request with green CI, then a squash merge and the branch deleted.

Branch names

<type>/DATA-<n>-<short-slug>, lower case, hyphens, the ticket key exactly as the Tracker shows it (its Copy branch name button does this for you).

feat/DATA-24-wms-orders-incremental
fix/DATA-61-dst-duplicate-hour
chore/DATA-38-ci-dbt-build
docs/DATA-87-on-time-definition
spike/DATA-12-platform-comparison

Commit messages

Conventional Commits with the ticket key at the end of the subject. Types: feat, fix, refactor, perf, test, docs, build, ci, chore. A ! after the type, or a BREAKING CHANGE: footer, marks a change that breaks a downstream consumer (a renamed column in a mart, a changed metric).

feat(wms): land order_line with hard-delete detection (DATA-24)
fix(silver): de-duplicate the repeated DST hour in task events (DATA-61)
feat(marts)!: rename on_time_flag to shipped_on_time (DATA-87)

BREAKING CHANGE: Warehouse Ops Scorecard must switch to the new column.

Pull requests and self-review

  • The PR title follows the commit convention; the body follows your template: ticket, what and why, how you tested, self-review checklist.
  • Review your own diff on GitHub, not in the editor, after a break. Comment on your own PR where a reviewer would ask “why”.
  • Move the ticket to In Review when the PR opens and to Done when it merges. Paste the PR link into the ticket.

CI on every pull request

  • Lint and format (Python and SQL), unit tests, secret scan.
  • Build and test the changed models against a development target, so a broken model never reaches main.
  • CI credentials live in GitHub Actions secrets or environments, scoped to development.

Secrets and personal data

  • Secrets live in .env (ignored) and in your platform’s secret store; .env.example lists the names only.
  • pre-commit runs gitleaks on every commit. Turn on GitHub’s secret scanning and push protection for the repository too.
  • A secret that reaches GitHub is leaked: rotate it first, then clean history.

Environments and infrastructure

  • Separate development and production (catalogs, schemas or databases). Production is changed only by a deploy from main.
  • Infrastructure as code for everything you create in the platform: warehouses or compute, catalogs and schemas, roles and grants, jobs. No click-ops that cannot be recreated.

Releases, CHANGELOG and ADRs

  • Each project ends with a release: tag v0.<project>.0 (v0.1.0 after Project 1, v1.0.0 after Project 8), patch tags for fixes. Create a GitHub release whose notes come from the CHANGELOG.
  • CHANGELOG.md in Keep a Changelog style: an Unreleased section you add to in every PR, moved under the version at release.
  • Architecture decision records in docs/adr/NNNN-title.md: context, options considered, decision, consequences. Accepted ADRs are not edited; a new ADR supersedes them.
git tag -a v0.1.0 -m "Project 1: data platform foundation"
git push origin v0.1.0
gh release create v0.1.0 --title "v0.1.0 · Data Platform Foundation" --notes-file release-notes.md

Working in the Tracker

The Tracker at localhost:8080/tracker works like Jira, the tool most data teams plan in. If Jira is new to you, this is what the words mean.

Project
All your work lives in project DATA; every ticket has a key such as DATA-24.
Epic
A large body of work with one outcome. Each of the eight projects is one epic.
Story
A milestone that delivers something a stakeholder can see, inside an epic. Stories carry story points.
Task, Bug, Spike
The work items inside a story, each with an hour estimate. A bug is a pre-written incident or stakeholder complaint that arrives on its date; a spike is a time-boxed investigation that ends in a decision. (In a Jira export they become sub-tasks.)
Sprint
A two-week time box. Each project is two sprints. You start a sprint from the backlog, work the board, then complete it; unfinished tickets move to the next sprint.
Backlog
Every ticket not yet in a sprint, in priority order.
Board
The current sprint’s tickets in columns: To Do, In Progress, In Review (your PR is open), Done (merged and verified). Drag cards across as you work. The Order switch shows each column by due date, overdue and soonest first.
Timeline
The eight epics laid out over 32 weeks.
Story points vs hours
Points size a story relative to other stories (an 8 is about twice a 3); they are for planning how much fits in a sprint. Hours estimate individual tasks. Your velocity is the points you complete per sprint; compare it with the plan.
Due date
Counted in working days from the start date you set. An overdue badge is the Tracker telling you to re-plan, not a failure.
Level
How hard a ticket is, shown as a small chip. Tickets start easy and get harder: Level 1 is the easiest, Level 5 the hardest.
Work log and comments
Log the hours you spend on each ticket and use comments as your engineering notes: decisions, dead ends, links. They become your case-study material.

Searching with JQL

JQL is Jira’s query language: field operator value, joined with AND or OR, then ORDER BY. Fields include project, type, status, priority, assignee, reporter, sprint, epic, labels, component, duedate, created and text; operators = != ~ < > <= >= IN; functions now(), startOfWeek(), endOfWeek().

status != Done AND duedate <= endOfWeek() ORDER BY duedate
type = Bug AND status != Done ORDER BY priority
text ~ "on-time" AND status != Done
labels IN (cdc, streaming) AND status = "In Progress"

A normal day

  1. Open the board. Pick the next ticket by due date and priority and move it to In Progress.
  2. Copy its branch name, create the branch, work, commit with the key.
  3. Open the PR, move the ticket to In Review, self-review, merge when CI is green, move it to Done.
  4. Log your hours and leave a comment with what you decided and why.

Shortcuts as in Jira: c creates a ticket, / searches, j and k move between issues. The Tracker can also export everything as a Jira CSV if you want to continue in a free Jira Cloud site.
